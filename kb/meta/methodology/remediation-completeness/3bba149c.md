# A remediation scoped to the instances you happened to observe is not complete until an independent full sweep confirms the observed set WAS the whole set — and the sweep informs only when it disagrees

When you fix a set of bad instances you noticed during an investigation, the fix silently inherits the scope of your observation: you repaired the ones you SAW, not the ones that EXIST. Nothing about the observed sample looks partial — a handful of genuine defects, each correctly identified and correctly fixed, reads as a finished job. That is exactly why the trap is invisible: there is no wrongness in the sample to notice.

Concrete case that earned this: the #132 ruling repaired 'the 6 observed self-refs' — self-referential lineage entries seen incidentally during an investigation, never the output of a systematic enumeration. A later full-corpus sweep found 42 self-refs on the write branch (plus 32 stale copies on main). The 6 were a sample read as a population, and the ruling's language ('the 6 observed') is the fossil of the scope error.

Consequence to act on: after remediating an OBSERVED set, do not treat the defect class as closed until a full independent enumeration of the population runs. And the enumeration carries information only when its count DISAGREES with your sample — agreement proves nothing, because a sweep that happens to match is indistinguishable from one that merely re-found what you already had. The disagreement is the entire signal, which is why the sweep is never redundant busywork even though it looks redundant right up until it isn't: it is the only instrument that can tell a complete remediation from a sample-scoped one.

Does NOT mean 'always sweep before fixing anything'. A fix explicitly scoped to 'these specific known instances' is honest and fine. The failure is specifically a fix that BELIEVES it closed the class while having only touched the incidentally-observed members of it.
